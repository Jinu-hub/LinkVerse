Not long ago, I reread the **AI 2027** scenario.

It is a forecast of the future published in April 2025 by researchers including Daniel Kokotajlo.

It describes AI agents automating coding, AI becoming deeply involved in AI research and accelerating progress even further,  
and eventually reaching a point where AI researchers more capable than humans develop the next generation of AI.

When I first encountered it, the scenario felt rather extreme.

Above all, 2027 felt far too close.

But rereading it now, in September 2026, feels a little different.

Of course, I cannot say that the scenario is becoming reality exactly as written.

Still, at least in terms of the direction it describes, the scenario feels closer to reality than I expected it would by now.

And that feeling does not come simply from seeing a lot of AI-related news.

Looking back at how I have worked over the past two years, the change has been significant.

## From 9:1 to 5:5, Then 1:9

Two years ago, if I had to put a ratio on how much of my coding was done by me versus AI, it would probably have been **9 parts me, 1 part AI**.

I asked AI questions and sometimes had it generate code, but fundamentally, I did the thinking and the implementation myself.

AI was certainly useful, but it was still just a tool that assisted with development.

About a year ago, the ratio reached roughly **5:5**.

I moved beyond simply asking about things I did not know and began handing a substantial part of feature implementation over to AI.

Even so, I still spent a lot of time writing code myself, and I retained the sense that I was at the center of the development process.

Now, though, the balance feels completely reversed.

**1 part me, 9 parts AI.**

I have worked as a developer for more than 15 years.

Even so, when it comes purely to the ability to write code,  
I now readily acknowledge that AI is better than I am in many areas.

In the past, I wrote the code and AI helped.

Now, I set the direction and AI implements it.

I think about what to build,
organize the requirements,
shape the structure,
provide the necessary information,
review what AI produces,
and correct it when it heads in the wrong direction.

The time I spend writing code line by line has noticeably decreased.

**9:1 → 5:5 → 1:9.**

This change happened in only about two years.

Compared with the more than 15 years I have spent working as a developer, it feels almost instantaneous.

That may be why rereading AI 2027 gave me a slight chill.

Not because I think the scenario’s timeline will prove exactly right,

but because the way I work already tells me that the pace of change is different from anything I have seen before.

## You Do Not Have to Be a Developer to Build Things Yourself

Recently, while taking part in the Open Claw challenge, I had a coffee chat with another participant.

As we talked about what we were building and the trial and error we had gone through, we arrived at an idea that stayed with me.

Someone who encounters friction in their work no longer needs to be an IT professional to try solving it themselves.

In the past, no matter how clearly someone in the field understood a problem, they still needed a developer to turn it into software.

The person doing the work would identify the problem,

explain it to a product manager or developer,

the developer would interpret the requirements,

design a solution,

implement it,

and then revise it after receiving feedback.

Now, the gap between identifying the problem and building a working tool is narrowing remarkably quickly.

You can explain your situation and frustration to AI,

saying, "I do this every day, and this part of the process is so inconvenient,"

and try building the tool you need on your own.

It does not have to be perfect from the start.

That is especially true when the tool is for personal use rather than a product to sell to others.

It is fine if the design is a little rough.

If you do not like where a button is placed, you can simply ask for it to be changed.

If an error occurs, you can show AI the error screen and ask it to fix the problem.

If a new need emerges while using the tool, you can add another feature.

By continuing that conversation with AI, you can gradually build a tool that is useful enough for you.

Of course, the moment you turn it into a product or service for other people, things become much more complicated.

There is a great deal to consider: security, operations, data, scalability, cost, incident response, and user experience.

But the standard is entirely different for **software for my own use**.

And in this area, the cost of creating software is falling quickly.

## Who Understands the Problem Best?

This naturally raises a question.

What, then, should developers do?

Until now, one of a developer’s key strengths was simple.

**The ability to build.**

The ability to turn an idea into working software was itself scarce.

But the person who best understands the real problems in a particular line of work is usually not a developer.

An accountant who does the work every day understands its frustrations better,

someone working at a logistics site understands the problems there better,

and the person running a small shop knows its repetitive tasks best.

Until now, developers bridged the gap because those people could understand the problem but could not build the solution themselves.

If AI continues to lower the barrier to building, that relationship may change as well.

If someone doing the work can structure their situation and problem clearly and communicate them to AI,

they may be able to build some kinds of software on their own.

And eventually, more people will realize this.

**"Wait, if something is inconvenient, can’t I just build a program that fits the way I work?"**

This is not yet a natural thought for everyone.

But once someone has experienced what that is like, it may be difficult to go back.

Instead of searching for the tool they need,

if it does not exist, they can simply build it.

## Where, Then, Does a Developer’s Strength Lie?

That does not mean I think a developer’s experience will suddenly lose its value.

Rather, I think where that value lies is changing.

Understanding a problem and expressing that problem as a good system are not the same thing.

For example, there is a significant difference between saying,

"Repeating this task every day is tedious,"

and explaining,

"This work proceeds in the order A → B → C, and B involves about 40 minutes of repetitive work every day. However, because there are three exceptions, it would be safer to automate only B rather than everything from A through C."

As AI becomes more capable of implementation, distinctions like this may become even more important.

The ability to identify the real problem.

The ability to structure a complex situation.

The ability to decide what to automate and what to leave to people.

The ability to connect multiple systems.

The ability to judge whether AI’s output is correct.

And the ability to turn it into a system that continues to run in a real environment.

My recent interest in and study of things like AI Agent, workflow, MCP, and n8n  
probably has something to do with this change.

My attention is gradually moving away from how to implement each piece of code,

and toward **how to put AI to work on real-world tasks**.

## What If We Design Organizations Instead of Programs?

Thinking a little further ahead, another change may emerge here.

If AI agents become capable of working independently for much longer periods than they can today,

we may find ourselves designing how multiple AIs work together rather than designing a single program.

One AI researches the material.

Another verifies it.

Another implements it.

And another evaluates the result.

The AIs handle unimportant decisions on their own,

while only risky or important judgments are escalated to a human.

Seen this way, it is closer to building a small organization than designing classes or functions.

I sometimes wonder whether the role we now call Software Engineer might eventually shift toward something like AI System Architect,  
or even AI Organization Designer.

Of course, I am not saying that jobs with those exact titles will actually appear.

I do not yet know exactly what comes next either.

Still, I cannot help wondering whether what we work with is gradually shifting

**from code to digital labor**.

## When the Ability to Build Is No Longer Scarce

So there is a question that has remained in my mind lately.

**If the ability to build is no longer scarce, what will become scarce instead?**

In the past, those who could build had the advantage.

Now, those who know how to get AI to build well are gaining the advantage.

In the future, perhaps

**the people who know what should be built**

will become more important.

Finding problems.

Judging whether a problem is truly worth solving.

Defining the desired outcome in concrete terms.

Deciding what to choose from the countless possibilities AI produces.

Perhaps the developer’s role will gradually shift in this direction as well.

But this, too, is only how I see things at this moment.

Given the pace of AI development, there is every chance I will be telling a completely different story a year from now.

## A Change We Have Not Yet Named

I have long believed that AI would change the world.

From the time I first began actively using AI, I expected a major shift to come.

But what I feel these days is a little different.

In the past, it felt as though a major change was coming in the future.

Now, it feels more like **we are already inside that change, but its next form is not yet clear**.

Two years ago, it was 9:1.

One year ago, it was 5:5.

Now, it is 1:9.

What will it look like a year from now?

Will it become 0:10?

Or by then, will talking about such a ratio no longer mean anything?

Perhaps the next change will not be about how much code people write,

but about a shift in the very meaning of what we have always taken for granted as **"building software."**

I still do not know.

That is precisely why I want to record this feeling now.

September 2026.

I can feel that something is definitely changing.

I cannot yet explain clearly what it is
or what we should do.

But when I read this again a few years from now,

**I think I will at least be able to say that, back then, I could already sense something was changing.**

That much feels worth leaving behind.
