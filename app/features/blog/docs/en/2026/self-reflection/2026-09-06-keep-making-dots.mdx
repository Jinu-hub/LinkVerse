Over the past year, I kept learning new things, building, pausing, and starting again.

I shipped a few services. Some are on hold now. For others, I am still exploring their potential.

The way I develop with AI has changed quite a bit, and running services myself showed me problems that development alone cannot solve.

So I wanted to look back at the past year from where I stand now.

What I learned. What I built. Where I got stuck.

And what I am doing now, and where I want to go next.

Rather than a grand retrospective, I made ten questions and answered them myself — as a way to organize my current thoughts.

## 1. What did you study over the past year?

If I look only at formal challenges, Open Claw is the second one I have taken part in, following the Maker Master Class a year ago.

Even so, I kept studying new technologies in between.

I learned how to compose AI Agents and connect tools and data,  
how to design multi-step automation pipelines with n8n, and how to connect AI to external capabilities through MCP.

Recently, I have also been building Agent structures myself on Cloudflare Workers — using State, Storage, and real-time communication.

Looking back, it feels less like I studied one technology in depth, and more like a year spent following how AI is changing the way we build software.

The projects I made afterward were shaped quite a lot by what I learned along the way.

## 2. What services have you built so far?

Over the past year, I shipped about three services.

The first one from the challenge is on hold for now.

After that, I built a service called `NexLetter`.

It collected conversations from multiple Slack channels, used AI to synthesize and summarize them into weekly reports, and sent those reports by email.

The service itself was reasonably complete, but the Slack App review took longer than expected. The momentum stalled, and the service is currently on hold.

Next is `Market Memory`, which I am still running.

When sources related to global markets are collected, an AI Agent and multiple n8n nodes work together to process the data and create reports.  
The results are stored in a database and presented on the web.

This service actually launched, and data is still accumulating.

But there is one big problem.

**Almost no one comes.**

I promote it occasionally through Threads and elsewhere, but I have not yet generated what I would call meaningful traffic.

## 3. Why do you keep writing a blog?

I still write a post on the blog about once a week.

It is less a professional tech blog and more a space for what I am building, the judgments I make while developing, personal thoughts and plans, and sometimes notes from daily life.

A large part of the reason I write is not so that someone else can read it right away, but so that I can come back to it later.

When I go back to older posts, I can still see what I was wrestling with then, and why I made certain choices.

Things that results alone cannot show remain in the record.

So I try to keep writing down the process, not only the finished outcomes.

## 4. What has been the hardest part of indie development?

I have always felt reasonably confident about development itself.

When a problem appears, I can find a way through. Even if it takes time, I believe I can eventually shape things the way I want.

But after building a service, a completely different problem was waiting.

**Building a product and getting people to know about it are entirely different skills.**

When I was making Market Memory, I thought that once Phase 1 delivered a basic service and some traffic started to appear,  
I would build Phase 2 with user feedback.

That expectation was quite wrong.

Before I could even think about Phase 2, bringing people in was already hard.

Creating content that looked worthwhile to me was not the same as creating something people actually needed.

This is the lesson I have been learning most clearly these days.

I never thought development alone would be enough — but I only really felt how wide the gap is after running a service myself.

## 5. Even so, what do you think went well?

One of the technical parts I am most satisfied with in Market Memory is the n8n pipeline.

I did not build one giant Workflow that handles everything.

There is a flow that takes source data and produces Core Data, and another that builds tags and Entity data — companies, people, technologies — from that.

Reports also go through different pipelines depending on their type.

And within each process, multiple Sub Workflows share the work.

The structure grew larger than I first designed,  
but the experience of making each pipeline move independently while still producing one result is something I expect to use a lot in other projects.

What I am less satisfied with is equally clear.

**What I think is good content may not be what people actually want.**

Improving the technical quality alone cannot solve this.

I am still looking for the answer here.

## 6. How much has AI changed the way you develop?

A year ago, the balance between AI writing code and me writing it myself felt roughly 5:5.

When AI wrote code, I checked it carefully, and I still rewrote a lot myself.

Now it is closer to 9:1.

I spend much less time watching every line.

I leave most of the implementation to AI. When needed, I review the structure or ask it to refactor the code.  
I spend more time making sure the direction is right in the important places, and that no strange flows have appeared.

In a sense, my role is drifting a little away from implementation itself.

Instead, judgment matters more — what to build, how to structure it, and how far to trust what AI produces.

With Open Claw, that change feels different again.

It is no longer only about writing code faster with AI, but about how to structure and operate the Agent itself.

## 7. What was the most interesting part of Open Claw?

The idea that an Agent on Cloudflare Workers can run when needed, only as much as needed, and then disappear, was personally quite interesting.

When combined with State, Storage, WebSocket, and Scheduling, even a small Agent can do more than you might expect.

What draws me especially is personalization.

Not showing every user the same screen and the same information,  
but remembering each person's interests and state, and presenting different information accordingly — I felt the structure was a good fit for that.

And I began to wonder whether this might connect to the place where Market Memory is currently stuck.

## 8. What do you most want to build right now?

Rather than starting another entirely new service, I want to connect what I am learning in Open Claw to Market Memory.

Market Memory already has many kinds of content and data.

I plan to start with the most basic and formal report, `Daily Global Market Issues`, and try presenting it in a new way.

For example, a very short summary of the day's market — **Today's 30-Second Briefing** — could be offered as text and audio.

Using the tags and Entity data accumulated so far, I could also show which companies, technologies, or topics have been appearing often lately.

Remembering a user's markets or topics of interest, and giving related content a bit more priority on the next visit, should also be possible.

As a more experimental idea, I am also thinking about a `Live Market Room`.

A space that activates only for a short window at a certain time, where people can see today's briefing and take part in simple votes or exchange opinions.

At this stage, I am still testing several possibilities.

But rather than simply listing existing data on a screen, I want to keep exploring ways to reframe it around each user's context.

## 9. Do you struggle between sticking with one thing and building something new?

A lot.

I have shipped three services so far, and two are currently on hold.

I sometimes wonder whether I should keep pouring everything into Market Memory alone, or whether it would be better to build something around a completely new idea.

Still, I do not think of the paused projects as failures I threw away.

The structure and automation experience from NexLetter carried into Market Memory as well.

The data structures and Agent patterns from Market Memory are also shaping how I look at Open Claw now.

Later, they may connect again in a completely different project.

So these days I think about it a little differently.

**For now, this is about continuing to make dots.**

I do not yet know which dots will connect where.

But if I had made nothing, there would be no dots to connect at all.

## 10. Will you keep doing indie development?

I think I probably will.

I plan to keep studying new technologies too.

Especially now, when things change quickly, simply following a new course or project often makes me look again at development habits I once took for granted.

For Market Memory, I want to hold on a bit longer rather than wrapping it up based only on short-term results.

Right now, traffic remains a large challenge, and there will clearly be parts where the direction needs to change.

If a new idea appears along the way, I might build another one as well.

That is how the past year went too.

Study, build, ship, pause, then build something else.

I still do not know which of those will eventually become a real result.

But one thing is a little clearer than before.

I do not need every dot I leave behind to be a success.

Even paused projects left experience behind, and services that went differently than expected still taught me something.

And those experiences are gradually feeding into what I build next.

Rather than saying I have finished something,

**I am still making dots.**

Even dots that look far apart for now may, when I look back someday, form one continuous line.

For now, that is enough reason to keep building.
