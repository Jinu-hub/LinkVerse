Recently, I have been running several experiments with AI Agents using Cloudflare Workers.

Rather than rebuilding Market Memory's existing data from scratch,  
my idea is to build a presentation layer as an Agent,  
allowing users to retrieve and interact with the accumulated data in a more natural way.

Along the way, I came across a service called `vooy`.

It is a Personal AI Agent that understands user requests inside familiar messaging apps like LINE, KakaoTalk, and Telegram,  
selects the necessary Tools, and follows through with real actions such as bookings, orders, and checking schedules.

At first, the service itself caught my interest. But looking a bit closer, something else caught my attention instead.

## A Service Not Yet Proven

vooy is not a solo project like mine.

Looking at their blog posts and event photos, a multi-person team seems to be working across research, development, marketing, and events.  
While I do not know the exact team size, it is clearly in a different weight class from solo development.

Yet on another level, it felt surprisingly familiar.

vooy is also not yet fully open to the general public.

They built a product, and they have a direction they believe in.  
In the end, though, whether that direction resonates with real users is something that remains to be seen.

No matter how good the technology is or how strong an idea feels, whether the market actually wants it is a separate question.

In that sense, regardless of team size, everyone stands before the very same question:

**Do people truly want what we've envisioned?**

I went through a similar experience while building Market Memory.

Producing functionally satisfying results did not automatically translate into user response.

These days, I often think that testing the hypotheses I set up is just as important as building more features.

## The Concept of Recipes

What interested me most while looking at vooy was `Recipe`.

If vooy itself is a general-purpose Personal Agent capable of using various Tools,  
a Recipe is closer to a feature where users teach that Agent how to behave to fit their own daily life or work.

For instance:

> On weekday mornings, check today's schedule. If there is an external meeting, calculate weather and travel time to tell me when I should leave.

Suppose someone has a request like this.

With traditional automation, you would have to wire together several pieces yourself — Calendar API, Scheduler, conditional branches, weather API, and messaging.

Even with tools like n8n or Make, you still need to understand automation structures to some degree.

But if Recipe Studio accepts such requests in natural language and configures a large share of the necessary Tools and execution steps, the dynamic changes completely.

From that point on, what matters is not how well you write code.

The weight shifts toward:

**What pain point do you notice in your own life or work?**

and

**Can you describe the flow for how you want to solve that problem?**

A great Recipe that developers never thought of might come from a parent raising children, a sales representative,  
a frequent traveler, or a restaurant owner.

After all, people understand their own problems best.

If it truly reaches that level of implementation, Recipe could become quite a fascinating concept.

## Why Host Recipe Day?

As it happens, vooy was also running an event called `Recipe Day` ahead of their official launch.

It is an event where participants use Recipe Studio directly to build an AI tailored to their work or daily life.

At first, I thought of it as simple product marketing.

Thinking about it again, though, from a product builder's perspective, it struck me as a remarkably good experiment.

Instead of a dev team imagining 100 use cases inside a conference room, they hand the tool to real people and say:

> Try building the AI you need.

Then Recipes the dev team never anticipated might emerge.

What professions show interest? What problems are people trying to automate?  
Where do they struggle with usage? Do they keep using the Recipes they made even after the event?

All of this turns into live product data.

In that sense, Recipe Day felt like marketing that doubles as an experiment very close to Product Discovery.

## More Interesting Now Than After Success

So going forward, I plan to follow vooy's moves step by step.

Analyzing services that have already succeeded is helpful, of course.

Once something succeeds, looking back makes almost every past decision seem deliberate and plausible.

Watching it unfold from this stage feels different:

You get to see what they emphasize right now.

Which features they choose to show first.

What kind of users they are trying to bring in.

What real users build that goes beyond initial expectations.

And you can watch how the product and its messaging evolve over time in response to those signals.

If it succeeds, I get to watch the process of success; if the market response turns out weaker than expected, I get to watch the process of course correction.

Either outcome offers meaningful lessons.

Going forward, when I look at vooy, I do not plan to just check whether new features were added.

Instead, I want to watch closely around four things:

### Hypothesis

What does this team believe will work right now?

### Experiment

How are they testing that belief with real users?

### Signal

What kind of response comes back from users?

### Change

How do they alter the product or messaging as a result?

Looking at Recipe right now, it can roughly be framed like this:

**Hypothesis**

Everyday users will want to create AI Agents tailored to their own lives and work, even without coding knowledge.

**Experiment**

Provide Recipe Studio, and have real users create their own Recipes through Recipe Day.

**Signal**

See what Recipes people build, where they struggle, and whether they actually use them repeatedly.

**Change**

Depending on the results, Recipe Studio's UX, Connectors, available features, and vooy's overall positioning may gradually shift.

Keeping notes like this over several months turns into more than just checking out an AI service — it becomes a record of observing a product find its place in the market from the sidelines.

## Questions to Ask Market Memory as Well

Watching this naturally leads me to ask questions about what I am building too.

So far with Market Memory, I mainly thought about structures where I define features and users consume them —  
showing today's market in 30 seconds,  
presenting data centered on assets of interest, or opening a Live Market Room at specific times.

Thinking a bit further, though, another direction is possible.

A user might say to the Agent:

> I mainly watch BTC, ETH, and AI-related companies.
> On weekday mornings, summarize only the issues likely to impact these assets first.
> If nothing special is happening, you don't need to notify me.

This configuration itself could become a personal Market Recipe.

Rather than designing every user experience in advance, it is a structure where users gradually shape the Agent to fit their own routines and interests.

I am not saying I will implement this right away.

Right now, I am still at the stage of experimenting with moving Market Memory's data presentation layer over to an Agent.

Watching vooy, however, added another question to how I view Agents:

**What can this Agent do?**

and beyond that,

**How easily can a user teach this Agent their own way of doing things?**

## Watching a Team Running a Little Ahead

vooy and I differ in scale and available resources.

Copying their methods directly would not make sense.

Still, there is plenty to learn from the questions they ask and how they go about testing them.

In fact, watching from a point where the results are not yet out might be the more interesting part.

Whether it is a team of twenty or a solo developer, everyone ultimately has to test their hypotheses against the market.

And that answer comes not from the person who wrote the code, but from the user.

Going forward, I plan to quietly watch how vooy launches officially and how Recipe is received by real users.

Rather than copying what I learn into my own project directly, I want to rethink those lessons in ways that fit my own context.

Neither side has a finished answer yet.

That is why keeping a record of these moves right now feels all the more meaningful.
